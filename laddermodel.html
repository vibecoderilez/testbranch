A field guide to the whole path

The Open Model Ladder

Say you want an AI that knows your domain cold: your codebase, your documentation, your house conventions, running on hardware you control instead of somebody's metered API. There is a well-worn path from "I have never done this" to a working custom model, and it is a ladder, not a leap. Each rung below covers what you actually do, what it costs, what tools people use, and what has come out of it in the wild. Most people climb until the problem is solved and then stop.

0

Run somebody else's weights

Weekend one · Ollama, LM Studio, llama.cpp

Pull a quantized open-weight model, run it on your own machine, and find out what a 7B actually feels like next to a 32B. This rung is not throat-clearing. It is where you learn quantization tradeoffs, context limits, and what your hardware can physically hold. Plenty of people discover here that a good base model already does 80% of what they wanted.

1

Prompt and retrieve before you train

Weeks one to four · RAG, system prompts, tool use

Put your documentation into a vector store, write a strict system prompt, wire up tool calls. This rung exists because fine-tuning teaches style and format far better than it teaches facts. Anything that changes belongs in retrieval, not in weights. People who skip this rung tend to come back to it after their first fine-tune confidently invents an API that does not exist.

What it gets you

A working agent, and something more valuable: a transcript log of every exchange that went well. That log is the raw material for rung 2. Start saving it on day one, or you will be paying to generate synthetic data later.

2

Supervised fine-tuning with LoRA / QLoRA

The rung where most personal models live and stay

Freeze the base model and train small low-rank adapter matrices on a thousand to five thousand prompt-and-response pairs, formatted as chat messages. QLoRA quantizes the frozen base to 4-bit first, which is the trick that put 32-billion-parameter training inside a single consumer graphics card. Rough rule of thumb from the Unsloth docs: parameter count in billions is roughly the GB of VRAM you need for QLoRA; plain 16-bit LoRA wants at least four times that.

What it gets you

A model that answers in your format, your idioms, your conventions, without a six-thousand-token system prompt on every single call. Export it to GGUF and it drops straight back into Ollama and into any editor that speaks a local API. The Dolphin and OpenHermes model families were built exactly this way and beat their base models on instruction-following benchmarks.

3

Preference alignment: DPO and friends

Once SFT works and the outputs are almost right

Supervised tuning teaches a model what a good answer looks like. Preference training teaches it which of two plausible answers is better, learned from pairs you rank yourself. DPO replaced the older reward-model-plus-PPO stack because it needs neither, and it is stable enough to run on the same rig as the previous rung. This is the step where a model stops producing output that is technically correct but somehow the wrong flavor.

What it gets you

Zephyr-7B is the canonical demonstration: Hugging Face took Mistral, ran SFT on synthetic data, then DPO on AI-ranked preferences, and matched chat models roughly ten times its size. No human annotators.

4

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

The current frontier for solo builders · GRPO

Instead of showing the model correct answers, you write a reward function and let it find its own path there. GRPO, from DeepSeek, dropped both the value model and the separate reward model, and that is what made this affordable outside a research lab. It only works when correctness is machine-checkable: does the code compile, does the test pass, does the JSON validate, is the arithmetic right. For anything code-shaped, that is a very natural fit.

What it gets you

Models that learn how an answer is derived rather than memorizing the answer itself, and that develop visible reasoning traces nobody explicitly taught them. The DeepSeek-R1 distills are the famous example. Individuals have reproduced small versions of the same effect on a single rented card.

5

Pretraining from scratch

Educational, mostly · and that's not an insult

Random weights, raw tokens, the entire pipeline. Almost nobody does this to obtain a useful model, because a hundred-dollar run buys you something roughly kindergarten-grade next to a base model you can download for free. People do it to understand the machine. Karpathy's nanochat is the reference implementation: tokenizer, pretraining, midtraining, supervised tuning, reinforcement learning and a chat interface in about eight thousand lines, trained end to end in roughly four hours on an eight-GPU node for about a hundred dollars.

The sideways rung: continued pretraining

Not higher, just different · sits beside rung 2

If your domain has vocabulary and structure the base model has genuinely never encountered, you can run further pretraining on raw domain text before any instruction tuning. It is the tool for teaching a model a language or a proprietary schema, not for teaching it how to behave. The failure mode is catastrophic forgetting, so people mix in general-purpose data and keep learning rates low. Most personal projects never need this rung; the ones that do usually know exactly why.

Patterns worth stealing

  • Data quality beats every other variable. A thousand hand-checked examples routinely outperform fifty thousand scraped ones. The people with good models are the people with good datasets, and they usually spent 80% of the project there.
  • Everyone stops earlier than they planned. The most common shape of a successful custom model is rung 1 plus rung 2, followed by years of quietly re-running rung 2 against a growing log.
  • Evaluate before you celebrate. Hold out ten percent of your data, and keep a fixed set of real tasks you rerun after every checkpoint. Evaluating by feel is how people ship a fine-tune that is quietly worse than the base model they started from.
  • Adapters are cheap, so branch freely. A LoRA adapter is a few hundred megabytes against a twenty-gigabyte base. Keeping five of them around and swapping between them is normal practice, and merging back down to GGUF is a final step rather than a commitment.
  • The loop is the product. Value compounds in the dataset, not in the weights. Weights get thrown out every time a better base model appears, and one always appears.
